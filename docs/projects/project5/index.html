Introduction

MaxDiff, also called Best-Worst Scaling, is a survey methodology for measuring how strongly people prefer one item over another. On each screen, the respondent sees a small subset of items — here, four — and selects the most-preferred and least-preferred item from that subset. They repeat this over many screens. That design choice matters: people are unreliable at calibrating numeric scales but generally good at making trade-offs. Forcing a best and a worst pick squeezes real differentiation out of each screen and avoids the scale-use bias that plagues 1–10 ratings. Sawtooth Software makes the same point about choice-based conjoint: survey tasks that mimic real-world trade-offs recover preferences more reliably than abstract ratings (Sawtooth Software, conjoint overview).

In this assignment, the 10 items are the 10 core MSBA classes at UCSD Rady. The goal is to estimate students’ relative preference for each class using three methods and to compare what each tells us:

  1. Counts analysis — percent of times each class was picked best minus percent of times picked worst.
  2. Multinomial logit (MNL) by maximum likelihood — an explicit random utility model of the best and worst choices, estimated with optim().
  3. Bayesian MNL — the same model, estimated with a hand-coded random-walk Metropolis-Hastings sampler.

The marketing context is bigger than “which class do students like?” Choice data are useful any time managers need to understand trade-offs — over price, product features, brand, or even people tied closely to the brand. A recent HBR article on CEOs becoming brands argues that when a leader’s identity becomes inseparable from the company’s brand, preference is no longer purely about product attributes: it also reflects identity signaling and reputational association (HBR, “When the CEO Becomes the Brand”). That framing applies directly here. Students choosing between class titles are not only evaluating technical content — they are also responding to instructor reputation and the social signal that preferring “Marketing Analytics” over “Analytics Consulting” sends about who they want to become professionally. A MaxDiff study that outputs only a ranking cannot disentangle these mechanisms, which is exactly why the Discussion section revisits what a richer preference model would need. Sawtooth’s pricing research note makes an analogous point: realistic choice tasks recover price sensitivity and willingness to pay better than simply asking people what they would pay (Sawtooth Software, pricing research).

My expectation is that the three methods will broadly agree. The counts approach is a useful first look, MLE MNL gives a formal model with classical standard errors, and the Bayesian model makes uncertainty on derived quantities — such as shares of preference — easy to report.

Counts Analysis

The counts analysis is the simplest way to score a MaxDiff study. For each class \(j\), I compute how often it was chosen as best — divided by how often it was shown — and how often it was chosen as worst. The score is:

\[ \text{score}_j = \%\text{best}_j - \%\text{worst}_j \]

A score near \(+1\) means the class is almost always picked best when shown; near \(-1\) means almost always picked worst; near \(0\) can mean middle-of-the-pack or a mix of strong love and strong dislike.

From MaxDiff Data to MNL Choices

The counts analysis is descriptive. The multinomial logit model is a model of the actual choice process. In a random utility framework, each class \(j\) has a latent utility \(\beta_j\). When a respondent sees a screen with four classes, the probability that class \(b\) is picked best is:

\[ P(\text{best}=b) = \frac{\exp(\beta_b)}{\sum_{j \in \text{shown}} \exp(\beta_j)} \]

The worst choice is modeled as a second logit decision over the remaining three items, with utilities flipped — the respondent is now choosing the least preferred:

\[ P(\text{worst}=w \mid \text{best}=b) = \frac{\exp(-\beta_w)}{\sum_{j \in \text{shown} \setminus b} \exp(-\beta_j)} \]

Each task therefore yields two MNL observations: the best-pick from the full shown set, and the worst-pick from the remaining set. This logic follows the discrete-choice foundation laid out in Train’s Discrete Choice Methods with Simulation, which introduces logit as a closed-form model of decision-makers choosing among alternatives and emphasizes an important identification point: only differences in utility matter, so utility has no natural absolute level. That is why I fix \(\beta_1 = 0\) and estimate the other nine coefficients relative to class 1.

Two modeling assumptions are important. First, this is a pooled MNL: every respondent is assumed to share the same class utilities. That is useful for a class-level ranking, but it ignores individual heterogeneity. Second, the MNL imposes the familiar logit/IIA structure, meaning substitution patterns are mechanically tied to relative utility. For that reason, the shares below should be read as model-implied shares of preference among these ten class titles, not literal enrollment demand or proof that students would make the same choices in the real registration environment.

MNL via Maximum Likelihood

For a task with shown set \(S\), best item \(b^*\), and worst item \(w^*\), the log-likelihood contribution is:

\[ \ell_t(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \beta_{b^*} - \log\!\!\sum_{j \in S}\!\exp(\beta_j) \;-\; \beta_{w^*} - \log\!\!\!\!\sum_{j \in S \setminus b^*}\!\!\!\exp(-\beta_j) \]

Summing over all tasks and estimating with optim():

Code 
ll <- function(theta, tasks = task_data) {
  beta  <- c(0, theta)   # pin beta_1 = 0; estimate beta_2:10
  total <- 0

  for (i in seq_len(nrow(tasks))) {
    shown     <- tasks$shown[[i]]
    best      <- tasks$best[i]
    worst     <- tasks$worst[i]
    remaining <- shown[shown != best]

    total <- total +
      beta[best]   - log_sum_exp( beta[shown]) +
      (-beta[worst]) - log_sum_exp(-beta[remaining])
  }
  total
}

# I minimize the negative log-likelihood rather than maximize ll() with
# fnscale = -1. That keeps the Hessian sign convention unambiguous:
# at the optimum, optim() returns the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood,
# so solve(H) is the classical covariance estimate for the free betas.
nll <- function(theta, tasks = task_data) -ll(theta, tasks)

mle_out <- optim(
  par     = rep(0, 9),

They answer the question, “If these ten course titles formed the relevant choice universe and everyone followed this average-preference MNL, how would preference mass be allocated?” That is still managerially useful, but it is narrower than real course demand. The delta-method confidence intervals here are directly comparable to the Bayesian credible intervals in the next section.

MNL via Bayesian Estimation

The Bayesian version uses the same likelihood, but multiplies it by a prior:

\[ \pi(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \cdot \pi(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \]

I use a weakly informative normal prior — SD \(\approx 3.2\) on each \(\beta\) is diffuse without being absurd:

\[ \boldsymbol{\beta}_{2:10} \sim N(\boldsymbol{0},\; 10 I) \]

I sample from the posterior with a hand-coded random-walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. To make the diagnostic story stronger, I run four chains from dispersed starting points around the MLE rather than relying on one chain.

Code 
log_post <- function(theta) {
  ll(theta, task_data) + sum(dnorm(theta, mean = 0, sd = sqrt(10), log = TRUE))
}

mh <- function(beta0, n_iter = 20000, step = 0.08) {
  K      <- length(beta0)
  draws  <- matrix(NA_real_, nrow = n_iter, ncol = K)
  colnames(draws) <- paste0("beta_", 2:10)
  beta   <- beta0
  lp     <- log_post(beta)
  accept <- 0

  for (t in seq_len(n_iter)) {
    beta_star <- beta + rnorm(K, mean = 0, sd = step)
    lp_star   <- log_post(beta_star)
    if (log(runif(1)) < lp_star - lp) {
      beta   <- beta_star
      lp     <- lp_star
      accept <- accept + 1
    }
    draws[t, ] <- beta
  }
  list(draws = draws, accept_rate = accept / n_iter)
}

I also compute effective sample size (ESS) for each parameter and a simple \(\hat{R}\) diagnostic across the four chains. ESS quantifies how many approximately independent draws the sampler produced after accounting for autocorrelation. \(\hat{R}\) compares within-chain and between-chain variation; values close to 1 indicate that the chains have reached the same posterior region.

Code 
ess_one <- function(x) {
  n  <- length(x)
  ac <- acf(x, lag.max = 500, plot = FALSE)$acf[-1, 1, 1]
  cutoff <- which(ac < 0)[1] - 1
  if (is.na(cutoff) || cutoff < 1) cutoff <- 1
  n / (1 + 2 * sum(ac[1:cutoff]))
}

rhat_one <- function(draws_by_chain) {
  n <- min(lengths(draws_by_chain))
  mat <- sapply(draws_by_chain, function(x) x[seq_len(n)])
  W <- mean(apply(mat, 2, var))
  B <- n * var(colMeans(mat))
  var_hat <- ((n - 1) / n) * W + (B / n)
  sqrt(var_hat / W)
}

The agreement does not mean the methods are interchangeable. The counts method is descriptive — it treats each pick independently and has no probability model behind it. Discussion

The substantive finding is unambiguous: MSBA students strongly prefer Marketing Analytics. It is the top class by all three methods. The next tier — Business Analytics, Customer Analytics, Large Data — is reasonably consistent. The lower tier — Analytics Consulting, Marketing/Finance/Operations, AI Math/Programming/Analytics — is where results are more tightly bunched and uncertainty is highest.

From a student advising perspective, the bottom of the list should not be read as “bad classes.” MaxDiff measures relative preference in a specific sample. A class can be valuable, required, or rigorous and still lose in a forced trade-off against one whose title sounds more career-relevant. Timing in the program, prior coursework, and instructor familiarity likely all affect responses.

This is where the HBR “CEO as brand” framing becomes analytically meaningful, but I use it cautiously. The article argues that when a person’s identity fuses with a brand, preference is no longer purely about functional attributes — it also reflects identity signaling and reputational association. In our data, the closest parallel is course-label and instructor identity: “Marketing Analytics” may win partly because of its topic, but also because the label carries strong identity-signal value to students thinking about their professional future. Conversely, “Analytics Consulting” may rank low not because consulting skills are unvalued, but because the label may resonate less with how early-program students picture themselves. This is a hypothesis, not something the current design identifies. A standard MaxDiff design that shows only course titles cannot separate content, instructor reputation, expected workload, scheduling, or career signaling. A richer study — analogous to how brand researchers add symbolic and experiential attributes alongside functional ones in a full conjoint — would need to include instructor profiles, sample deliverables, workload descriptions, and alumni outcome data as explicit attributes.

For method selection, the three approaches serve different jobs:

  • Counts for a fast descriptive summary or executive dashboard — transparent, assumption-free, but not a probability model.
  • MLE MNL when you need rigorous point estimates with classical standard errors and delta-method CIs; good for a single clean number to report.
  • Bayesian MNL when you want uncertainty on derived quantities (shares, market simulations, “what if we added a new class?” scenarios), or when you plan to extend the model hierarchically to estimate individual-level preferences.

Given more time, I would extend in two directions. First, estimate segment-level preferences to test whether students cluster into “technical,” “marketing/customer,” and “project/capstone” preference groups. Second, add instructor-identity and course-outcome attributes — informed by the HBR framing — to test whether symbolic associations drive preference independently of course content. That would turn this from a class ranking into a richer preference segmentation problem with real implications for curriculum design and student advising.

## Introduction

MaxDiff, also called Best-Worst Scaling, is a survey methodology for measuring how strongly people prefer one item over another. On each screen, the respondent sees a small subset of items — here, four — and selects the most-preferred and least-preferred item from that subset. They repeat this over many screens. That design choice matters: people are unreliable at calibrating numeric scales but generally good at making trade-offs. Forcing a best and a worst pick squeezes real differentiation out of each screen and avoids the scale-use bias that plagues 1–10 ratings. Sawtooth Software makes the same point about choice-based conjoint: survey tasks that mimic real-world trade-offs recover preferences more reliably than abstract ratings ([Sawtooth Software, conjoint overview](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/conjoint-analysis)).

In this assignment, the 10 items are the 10 core MSBA classes at UCSD Rady. The goal is to estimate students' relative preference for each class using three methods and to compare what each tells us:

1. **Counts analysis** — percent of times each class was picked best minus percent of times picked worst.
2. **Multinomial logit (MNL) by maximum likelihood** — an explicit random utility model of the best and worst choices, estimated with `optim()`.
3. **Bayesian MNL** — the same model, estimated with a hand-coded random-walk Metropolis-Hastings sampler.

The marketing context is bigger than "which class do students like?" Choice data are useful any time managers need to understand trade-offs — over price, product features, brand, or even people tied closely to the brand. A recent HBR article on CEOs becoming brands argues that when a leader's identity becomes inseparable from the company's brand, preference is no longer purely about product attributes: it also reflects identity signaling and reputational association ([HBR, "When the CEO Becomes the Brand"](https://hbr.org/2026/04/when-the-ceo-becomes-the-brand)). That framing applies directly here. Students choosing between class titles are not only evaluating technical content — they are also responding to instructor reputation and the social signal that preferring "Marketing Analytics" over "Analytics Consulting" sends about who they want to become professionally. A MaxDiff study that outputs only a ranking cannot disentangle these mechanisms, which is exactly why the Discussion section revisits what a richer preference model would need. Sawtooth's pricing research note makes an analogous point: realistic choice tasks recover price sensitivity and willingness to pay better than simply asking people what they would pay ([Sawtooth Software, pricing research](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/resources/blog/posts/conjoint-analysis-pricing-research)).

My expectation is that the three methods will broadly agree. The counts approach is a useful first look, MLE MNL gives a formal model with classical standard errors, and the Bayesian model makes uncertainty on derived quantities — such as shares of preference — easy to report.

## Counts Analysis

The counts analysis is the simplest way to score a MaxDiff study. For each class $j$, I compute how often it was chosen as best — divided by how often it was shown — and how often it was chosen as worst. The score is:

$$
\text{score}_j = \%\text{best}_j - \%\text{worst}_j
$$

A score near $+1$ means the class is almost always picked best when shown; near $-1$ means almost always picked worst; near $0$ can mean middle-of-the-pack or a mix of strong love and strong dislike.

## From MaxDiff Data to MNL Choices

The counts analysis is descriptive. The multinomial logit model is a model of the actual choice process. In a random utility framework, each class $j$ has a latent utility $\beta_j$. When a respondent sees a screen with four classes, the probability that class $b$ is picked best is:

$$
P(\text{best}=b) = \frac{\exp(\beta_b)}{\sum_{j \in \text{shown}} \exp(\beta_j)}
$$

The worst choice is modeled as a second logit decision over the remaining three items, with utilities *flipped* — the respondent is now choosing the least preferred:

$$
P(\text{worst}=w \mid \text{best}=b) = \frac{\exp(-\beta_w)}{\sum_{j \in \text{shown} \setminus b} \exp(-\beta_j)}
$$

Each task therefore yields *two* MNL observations: the best-pick from the full shown set, and the worst-pick from the remaining set. This logic follows the discrete-choice foundation laid out in Train's *Discrete Choice Methods with Simulation*, which introduces logit as a closed-form model of decision-makers choosing among alternatives and emphasizes an important identification point: only *differences* in utility matter, so utility has no natural absolute level. That is why I fix $\beta_1 = 0$ and estimate the other nine coefficients relative to class 1.

Two modeling assumptions are important. First, this is a **pooled** MNL: every respondent is assumed to share the same class utilities. That is useful for a class-level ranking, but it ignores individual heterogeneity. Second, the MNL imposes the familiar logit/IIA structure, meaning substitution patterns are mechanically tied to relative utility. For that reason, the shares below should be read as **model-implied shares of preference among these ten class titles**, not literal enrollment demand or proof that students would make the same choices in the real registration environment.

## MNL via Maximum Likelihood

For a task with shown set $S$, best item $b^*$, and worst item $w^*$, the log-likelihood contribution is:

$$
\ell_t(\boldsymbol{\beta}) =
\beta_{b^*} - \log\!\!\sum_{j \in S}\!\exp(\beta_j)
\;-\; \beta_{w^*} - \log\!\!\!\!\sum_{j \in S \setminus b^*}\!\!\!\exp(-\beta_j)
$$

Summing over all tasks and estimating with `optim()`:

```{r mnl-log-likelihood}
ll <- function(theta, tasks = task_data) {
  beta  <- c(0, theta)   # pin beta_1 = 0; estimate beta_2:10
  total <- 0

  for (i in seq_len(nrow(tasks))) {
    shown     <- tasks$shown[[i]]
    best      <- tasks$best[i]
    worst     <- tasks$worst[i]
    remaining <- shown[shown != best]

    total <- total +
      beta[best]   - log_sum_exp( beta[shown]) +
      (-beta[worst]) - log_sum_exp(-beta[remaining])
  }
  total
}

# I minimize the negative log-likelihood rather than maximize ll() with
# fnscale = -1. That keeps the Hessian sign convention unambiguous:
# at the optimum, optim() returns the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood,
# so solve(H) is the classical covariance estimate for the free betas.
nll <- function(theta, tasks = task_data) -ll(theta, tasks)

mle_out <- optim(
  par     = rep(0, 9),
  fn      = nll,
  method  = "BFGS",
  hessian = TRUE,
  control = list(maxit = 10000)
)

mle_out$convergence   # 0 = converged
-mle_out$value        # maximized log-likelihood
```

## MNL via Bayesian Estimation

The Bayesian version uses the same likelihood, but multiplies it by a prior:

$$
\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta} \mid \boldsymbol{y}) \propto L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \cdot \pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})
$$

I use a weakly informative normal prior — SD $\approx 3.2$ on each $\beta$ is diffuse without being absurd:

$$
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2:10} \sim N(\boldsymbol{0},\; 10 I)
$$

I sample from the posterior with a hand-coded random-walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. To make the diagnostic story stronger, I run four chains from dispersed starting points around the MLE rather than relying on one chain.

```{r bayes-mh}
log_post <- function(theta) {
  ll(theta, task_data) + sum(dnorm(theta, mean = 0, sd = sqrt(10), log = TRUE))
}

mh <- function(beta0, n_iter = 20000, step = 0.08) {
  K      <- length(beta0)
  draws  <- matrix(NA_real_, nrow = n_iter, ncol = K)
  colnames(draws) <- paste0("beta_", 2:10)
  beta   <- beta0
  lp     <- log_post(beta)
  accept <- 0

  for (t in seq_len(n_iter)) {
    beta_star <- beta + rnorm(K, mean = 0, sd = step)
    lp_star   <- log_post(beta_star)
    if (log(runif(1)) < lp_star - lp) {
      beta   <- beta_star
      lp     <- lp_star
      accept <- accept + 1
    }
    draws[t, ] <- beta
  }
  list(draws = draws, accept_rate = accept / n_iter)
}

# Four chains from dispersed starts give a more honest convergence check than
# a single chain. The sampler itself is still intentionally hand-coded.
n_chains <- 4
n_iter   <- 20000
step_sd  <- 0.08

set.seed(20260528)

  mle_out$par + rnorm(length(mle_out$par), mean = 0, sd = 0.20),
  simplify = FALSE

I also compute **effective sample size (ESS)** for each parameter and a simple $\hat{R}$ diagnostic across the four chains. ESS quantifies how many approximately independent draws the sampler produced after accounting for autocorrelation. $\hat{R}$ compares within-chain and between-chain variation; values close to 1 indicate that the chains have reached the same posterior region.

```{r ess-autocorr}
ess_one <- function(x) {
  n  <- length(x)
  ac <- acf(x, lag.max = 500, plot = FALSE)$acf[-1, 1, 1]
  cutoff <- which(ac < 0)[1] - 1
  if (is.na(cutoff) || cutoff < 1) cutoff <- 1
  n / (1 + 2 * sum(ac[1:cutoff]))
}

rhat_one <- function(draws_by_chain) {
  n <- min(lengths(draws_by_chain))
  mat <- sapply(draws_by_chain, function(x) x[seq_len(n)])
  W <- mean(apply(mat, 2, var))
  B <- n * var(colMeans(mat))
  var_hat <- ((n - 1) / n) * W + (B / n)
  sqrt(var_hat / W)
}

ess_vals <- map_dbl(seq_len(ncol(post)), function(k) {
  sum(map_dbl(post_by_chain, ~ ess_one(.x[, k])))
})

rhat_vals <- map_dbl(seq_len(ncol(post)), function(k) {
  rhat_one(map(post_by_chain, ~ .x[, k]))

## Discussion

The substantive finding is unambiguous: MSBA students strongly prefer **Marketing Analytics**. It is the top class by all three methods. The next tier — Business Analytics, Customer Analytics, Large Data — is reasonably consistent. The lower tier — Analytics Consulting, Marketing/Finance/Operations, AI Math/Programming/Analytics — is where results are more tightly bunched and uncertainty is highest.

From a student advising perspective, the bottom of the list should not be read as "bad classes." MaxDiff measures relative preference in a specific sample. A class can be valuable, required, or rigorous and still lose in a forced trade-off against one whose title sounds more career-relevant. Timing in the program, prior coursework, and instructor familiarity likely all affect responses.

This is where the HBR "CEO as brand" framing becomes analytically meaningful, but I use it cautiously. The article argues that when a person's identity fuses with a brand, preference is no longer purely about functional attributes — it also reflects identity signaling and reputational association. In our data, the closest parallel is course-label and instructor identity: "Marketing Analytics" may win partly because of its topic, but also because the label carries strong identity-signal value to students thinking about their professional future. Conversely, "Analytics Consulting" may rank low not because consulting skills are unvalued, but because the label may resonate less with how early-program students picture themselves. This is a hypothesis, not something the current design identifies. A standard MaxDiff design that shows only course titles cannot separate content, instructor reputation, expected workload, scheduling, or career signaling. A richer study — analogous to how brand researchers add symbolic and experiential attributes alongside functional ones in a full conjoint — would need to include instructor profiles, sample deliverables, workload descriptions, and alumni outcome data as explicit attributes.

For method selection, the three approaches serve different jobs:

- **Counts** for a fast descriptive summary or executive dashboard — transparent, assumption-free, but not a probability model.
- **MLE MNL** when you need rigorous point estimates with classical standard errors and delta-method CIs; good for a single clean number to report.
- **Bayesian MNL** when you want uncertainty on derived quantities (shares, market simulations, "what if we added a new class?" scenarios), or when you plan to extend the model hierarchically to estimate individual-level preferences.

Given more time, I would extend in two directions. First, estimate segment-level preferences to test whether students cluster into "technical," "marketing/customer," and "project/capstone" preference groups. Second, add instructor-identity and course-outcome attributes — informed by the HBR framing — to test whether symbolic associations drive preference independently of course content. That would turn this from a class ranking into a richer preference segmentation problem with real implications for curriculum design and student advising.
